Argument 1 of 6

Abolition relocates selection from the fee to the postcode

Intrinsic score 8.4 — ranked 1st of 6. Truth 8 · Relevance 9 · Logic 8 · Importance of impact 9. Weakest stage: evidence.

Claim. Abolishing private schools does not remove selective education. It moves the point of selection from the fee to the postcode, and postcodes are far harder to tax than fees.

Mechanism. Families who currently convert money into educational advantage through tuition do not stop wanting to convert it once tuition is illegal; they look for the nearest available market, and in the UK that market is housing. State secondary admissions are overwhelmingly decided by distance from the gate, so a house inside the catchment of a strong comprehensive is a direct substitute for a term's fees. Roughly six hundred thousand privately educated pupils enter that market at once, bidding against families already in it and against each other. The catchment premium rises, and it rises fastest around exactly the schools that were already oversubscribed.

Evidence. Homes inside the catchment of high-performing English state secondaries already sell at a measurable premium over near-identical homes a street outside, and the same pattern appears wherever places are allocated by distance rather than by test.

Impact. The comparative is worse for the people the motion is meant to help. A fee is visible, annual, and something a government can tax, cap or regulate; a house price is none of those. It capitalises into the wealth of families who already own, locks in for a generation, and stays invisible to education policy because it is not education spending. Selection survives the motion, the state loses the only lever it had over it, and the poorest child now competes against a mortgage instead of against a bursary.
