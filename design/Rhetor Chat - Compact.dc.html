1. Abolition relocates selection from the fee to the postcode

Provisionally your first argument. Not yet scored; the evidence stage is the weakest part.

Claim. Abolishing private schools does not remove selective education. It moves the point of selection from the fee to the postcode, and postcodes are far harder to tax than fees.

Mechanism. Families who currently convert money into educational advantage through tuition do not stop wanting to convert it once tuition is illegal; they look for the nearest available market, and in the UK that market is housing. State secondary admissions are overwhelmingly decided by distance from the gate, so a house inside the catchment of a strong comprehensive is a direct substitute for a term's fees. Roughly six hundred thousand privately educated pupils enter that market at once, bidding against families already in it and against each other.

Evidence. Homes inside the catchment of high-performing English state secondaries already sell at a measurable premium over near-identical homes a street outside.

Impact. A fee is visible, annual, and something a government can tax, cap or regulate; a house price is none of those. It capitalises into the wealth of families who already own, locks in for a generation, and stays invisible to education policy because it is not education spending. Selection survives the motion, and the poorest child now competes against a mortgage instead of against a bursary.

2. The bursary pipeline collapses before the state absorbs it

Provisionally second. Not yet scored; drafted with you in the chat.

Claim. The children the motion is meant to help are the ones it strands first, because bursaries end on day one and state capacity arrives years later.

Mechanism. Around a third of pupils at large independent schools receive some fee remission, funded out of full-fee income and endowment. Abolition removes the income before it removes the demand: the school closes, the bursary ends immediately, and the pupil returns to a state sector that has not yet built the place, hired the teacher, or received the redirected money. Fiscal transfers move on budget cycles; a closure moves in a term.

Evidence. Needs a figure on independent-sector bursary spend and the lag between a school closure and local state provision expanding.

Impact. The cohort that absorbs the transition cost is the bursary cohort — low-income pupils already in the system — and they absorb it during exam years they cannot repeat.
