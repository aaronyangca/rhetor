# Rhetor Run: Government Case

**Motion:** *This House Supports ghosting.*
**Definition (given):** "Ghosting" refers to abruptly cutting off contact with someone without giving the other person any warning or explanation for doing so.

**Side being prepared:** Government — defends ghosting as a legitimate, defensible practice. **Bench position assumed:** Opening Government (OG).

**Definitional scope note (not fiat — a model-setting choice):** The motion doesn't specify a relationship type, and the strongest, most representative version of this debate is the casual/early-stage/low-investment case — app-based dating, brief acquaintances, situationships, first few dates — since that's the modal case people mean when they say "ghosting" and where the practice is actually contested. Government runs the case on that ground rather than stretching to defend silently vanishing on a ten-year spouse, without conceding the practice is *never* legitimate outside it.

**Factual caveat:** several Evidence items below rest on general, well-documented patterns (dating-safety guidance, emotional-labor concept, digital dating-volume shift) rather than a specific cited study — each is flagged with its actual confidence level rather than asserted with false precision.

---

## Motion Classification

- **Axis 1 — Structural type:** THS, grouped with THBT/THO. Government's burden: show that ghosting is, comparatively, a justified practice — not that it's flawless, just that it beats the realistic alternative (a norm requiring an explicit explanation before disengaging).
- **Fiat:** None. Nothing is implemented by winning the debate; no future norm-change is caused by the round's outcome. Every Mechanism and Impact below is grounded in what's already true about the two practices as they stand, not in a claim that winning makes anyone adopt anything.
- **Axis 2 — Topic domain:** No single map category is a precise fit for interpersonal disengagement conduct. Frames pulled primarily from Social Policy (paternalism/autonomy, harm premise) and Labour (dignity/allocation of labour, applied here to emotional rather than economic labour); Science/Technology's "scale" logic borrowed for the dating-app-volume argument. Legal domain: attempted, effectively empty — the closest thing found is the narrow power-imbalance case (Argument H below).

### 1. No Standing Obligation
- **Claim:** Nobody owes another person a justification for ending contact, because continued contact runs on ongoing consent from both sides, not a one-time grant that persists until formally revoked.
- **Mechanism:**
  - Contact between two people is voluntary at every moment, not a binding commitment created by the first message
  - An obligation to justify withdrawal has to come from somewhere — an explicit prior promise (rare) or an implied one from continued engagement (contested, and not the default)
  - Under the harm premise, a demand backed by social sanction is only legitimate if silence causes a harm serious enough to override the withdrawing party's freedom to disengage
  - Ambiguity discomfort is a cost of uncertainty, not a harm inflicted by an act — it doesn't clear that bar the way active deception or continued unwanted pursuit would
- **Evidence:** Applying harm-premise reasoning to informal interpersonal conduct rather than state coercion is a standard extension of a recognized ethical framework. *Confidence: well-established as a framework; its specific application to social (non-legal) obligation is the debate's own normative question, not a settled fact.*
- **Second-Order Effect:** If ambiguity-discomfort alone justified an obligation to explain, the same logic would generalize to obligate justification for ending any voluntary low-commitment contact — declining a second coffee, not returning a casual acquaintance's message — a scope of obligation neither side actually holds people to elsewhere.
- **Impact:** Breadth: the default state of every voluntary low-commitment relationship, not a narrow edge case. Comparative: sets the round's actual burden of proof — Opposition must show a harm clearing Mill's bar, not just that ambiguity is unpleasant; absent that, the practice stands justified by default.

### 2. Silence Is the Only Screening That Scales *(core)*
- **Claim:** Because no discloser can reliably identify in advance which recipients will react to rejection with anger or escalation, silent disengagement is the only version of ending contact that protects against that risk across a whole population of low-information contacts.
- **Mechanism:**
  - A minority of people, when explicitly rejected, respond with anger, guilt-tripping, escalating messages, or — more rarely — stalking or harassment
  - Early-stage, low-information contacts (an app match, someone met once or twice) don't give the discloser enough information to identify that minority in advance
  - Because the risky subset can't be filtered out beforehand, any "always explain" policy necessarily delivers the triggering message to that subset too, not just to the safe majority
  - Silent disengagement is the only version of the practice that never delivers that triggering stimulus, because it never singles out or directly confronts anyone
- **Evidence:**
  - Documented escalation of stalking or harassment following an explicit rejection is a recognized pattern in personal-safety and domestic-violence-prevention literature aimed at online daters. *Confidence: well-established as a documented phenomenon; not majority-likely per encounter, but real and unpredictable enough to be the mechanism's point.*
  - Personal-safety guidance for people leaving a partner showing controlling behaviour commonly favours a quiet exit over a confrontational one. *Confidence: well-established general advice; exact sourcing not confirmable from memory.*
- **Second-Order Effect:** Because the risky subset is exactly what an "always explain" norm can't filter out in advance, mandating explanation as a general rule concentrates risk precisely where an OIV would least want it concentrated — on people who have no way to know they're talking to the exception.
- **Impact:** Breadth: everyone navigating low-information initial contact, a large and growing share of how relationships now begin (see Argument 5). Depth: for the affected minority, potentially severe — harassment, stalking, or physical harm. Probability: low per individual encounter, but unknowable in advance, which is the mechanism's entire point. Reversibility: the harm avoided (violence, sustained harassment) is far less reversible than the harm it costs (ambiguity discomfort). Comparative: the sharpest asymmetry in the round — a certain, mild, universal discomfort traded against an unpredictable but severe risk to an unidentifiable subset.

### 3. The Explanation Tax Falls on the Wrong Party
- **Claim:** Requiring an explanation puts the disengaging party back to work managing the other person's feelings — often the same unwanted emotional labour, directed at the same person, that motivated them to withdraw in the first place.
- **Mechanism:**
  - In the disputed cases, disengagement frequently follows from the contact having become effortful, unwanted, or one-sided — an over-eager match, a persistent acquaintance not reading earlier soft signals
  - An "always explain" norm requires that same discloser to compose a message calibrated to be honest without being needlessly cruel, anticipate the reaction, and often manage a follow-up
  - That composition and anticipation work is itself the same kind of emotional labour — managing someone else's feelings about a decision that isn't theirs to make — the discloser was trying to exit
  - The norm doesn't remove the imbalance that motivated disengagement; it extends it by one more round, and bills it to the person who already wanted out
- **Evidence:** The general concept that carefully composing a difficult message to manage someone else's reaction constitutes real emotional labour is consistent with broader research on emotional labour in interpersonal communication. *Confidence: plausible-but-unverified as a direct citation — a structural inference from a well-established general concept, not a study of this specific scenario.*
- **Second-Order Effect:** Because the anticipated cost of the conversation (managing the reaction) is what makes people avoid it, an enforced norm doesn't reliably produce more honest conversations — it produces more indefinitely postponed ones, since the incentive to avoid the costly conversation doesn't disappear because a norm says it should happen.
- **Impact:** Breadth: the modal disputed case — where one party invested more interest than the other. Depth: moderate but compounding — repeated across many low-investment contacts, this labour accumulates specifically on whichever party is already more socially burdened by being pursued. Comparative: identifies who actually pays for the "explanation" norm, and shows it isn't neutrally distributed — it's a second helping of exactly the imbalance in dispute.

### 4. Silence Reads Truer Than a Soft No *(core flip)*
- **Claim:** Because a demanded explanation is, in practice, usually a softened and partly dishonest account, requiring one doesn't give the other person more truth — it trades honest ambiguity for false certainty that keeps hope alive longer than silence does.
- **Mechanism:**
  - When people do give a reason for ending contact, they routinely soften it to avoid conflict or guilt — "it's not you, it's timing," "let's stay friends," "I've just been busy" — rather than the true, often more mundane or unflattering reason
  - These softened explanations are chosen specifically to reduce the sting, which means they frequently signal more possibility of future contact than actually exists
  - A recipient who receives a soft, hedged explanation has rational grounds to read it as leaving room for hope, because that's exactly what the wording is designed to do
  - Silence contains no such false signal — it's unpleasant precisely because it's unresolved, but the recipient isn't being actively misled about the odds of reconciliation, and most people competently read it as a firm no once enough time passes
- **Evidence:** Socially "softening" a rejection with hedged language or a small dishonesty to avoid confrontation is a widely recognised feature of ordinary social communication. *Confidence: well-established as an observed pattern; that it is the typical mode of delivered explanations, rather than merely a common one, is a plausible-but-unverified extrapolation.*
- **Second-Order Effect:** A recipient strung along by a hope-preserving soft explanation is likely to invest further time and emotional energy pursuing a possibility that was never real — a more concrete, compounding harm than the ambiguity ghosting leaves behind.
- **Impact:** Breadth: every case where the true reason is unflattering enough that most people soften rather than state it plainly — the modal case in casual/early-stage disengagement. Depth: moderate-high — prolonged false hope compounds; unresolved silence is front-loaded and typically resolves once enough time passes without contact. Comparative: directly denies Opposition's likely core premise that the explanation-owed norm leaves the disengaged party better informed — it argues the opposite, that it leaves them confidently wrong instead of honestly uncertain.

### 5. The Norm Doesn't Scale to Modern Contact Volume
- **Claim:** A norm obligating a personalised explanation for every non-continuing contact was built for a world of low-volume courtship, and applying it at the volume modern dating platforms generate functionally denies people the standing to end low-investment contact at anything less than disproportionate cost.
- **Mechanism:**
  - Dating and casual social platforms generate a volume of low-investment initial contacts far exceeding what pre-digital courtship norms — where the "always explain" expectation took shape — were ever built to handle
  - Requiring a personalised, thoughtful explanation for each non-continuing contact multiplies a real per-contact cost (time, composition effort, the risk managed in Argument 2, the labour in Argument 3) by that much higher volume
  - Once multiplied across realistic modern volume, the aggregate cost of full compliance becomes disproportionate to the stakes of most individual low-investment contacts
  - Because the norm doesn't scale, insisting on it doesn't just make disengagement mildly less convenient — it raises the cost of exiting so high that people either burn out complying or find some workaround the norm doesn't actually prevent
- **Evidence:** An order-of-magnitude increase in the number of initial romantic/social contacts enabled by swipe-based dating platforms compared to pre-app courtship is a widely observed feature of contemporary dating culture. *Confidence: well-established as a qualitative pattern; exact volume figures not cited from memory, treated as structural inference.*
- **Second-Order Effect:** If the "always explain" norm survives only because most people quietly don't comply with it at true scale — which its continued prevalence suggests — its actual function isn't delivering universal closure, it's supplying a standard people can be shamed by for failing to meet, with no achievable path to meeting it.
- **Impact:** Breadth: the entire population of low-investment digital-era contact — now a large share of how new contact begins. Comparative: the pool's clearest "why now" argument — it doesn't require ghosting to be timelessly fine, only that a norm workable at pre-digital courtship volume doesn't survive being applied at today's volume, which is the world both sides are actually debating.

### 6. Confrontation Attempts Often Wound More Than Silence
- **Claim:** When someone anxious about the conversation attempts an explicit rejection anyway, the result is frequently clumsier and more hurtful than if they had simply stopped responding, because discomfort tends to produce poorly chosen words, not careful ones.
- **Mechanism:**
  - People uncomfortable giving an explanation often delay it under stress, then deliver it hastily once cornered rather than composing it carefully
  - Under that stress, common failure modes include blurting an unnecessarily harsh true reason, over-explaining in a way that reveals more judgment than intended, or fumbling a hedge that reads as contradictory or cold
  - A clumsy, stress-produced explanation often lands as more pointed and personal than ambiguous silence, because it supplies specific, sometimes harsh content to fixate on rather than an absence to eventually stop checking
  - Forcing the attempt doesn't improve the discloser's comfort or skill at the conversation — it just guarantees an attempt happens, regardless of how well it goes
- **Evidence:** General communication research on how acute social anxiety degrades message quality in high-stakes interpersonal exchanges supports this pattern. *Confidence: plausible-but-unverified as a direct citation — a structural inference from well-established anxiety/communication-quality research, not a study of this specific scenario.*
- **Second-Order Effect:** None.
- **Impact:** Breadth: conflict-avoidant dischargers, a substantial subset of the population, not a fringe case. Depth: moderate — a harsh or clumsy explanation can be more acutely painful in the moment than ambiguity, even if ambiguity's discomfort lasts longer. Comparative: undercuts the assumption that mandating explanation reliably produces a better experience for the recipient than silence would have.

### 7. Mutual Relief From an Unwanted Conversation *(STANDARD)*
- **Claim:** Ghosting spares both people a conversation that, in the disputed cases, neither one actually wants to have.
- **Mechanism:**
  - In the modal low-investment disputed case, both parties often sense the contact isn't continuing before any words are exchanged
  - Stating that explicitly forces a moment of acknowledged rejection that can be awkward for the person delivering it and, in some cases, uncomfortable for the person receiving it, beyond what a quiet fade would produce
  - Silent disengagement lets both parties exit without either being made to sit through that acknowledgment
- **Evidence:** General social observation about mutual awkwardness in explicit rejection conversations. *Confidence: plausible-but-unverified, low-specificity — closer to a widely shared intuition than a documented finding.*
- **Second-Order Effect:** None.
- **Impact:** Breadth: broad but shallow — the mutual-discomfort dimension of many low-investment cases. Depth: mild. Comparative: real but modest; mostly table-setting rather than differentiating weighing material, and the thinnest-evidenced argument in the pool.

---

### 8. Explanation Can Be Leveraged Against the Discloser *(low-confidence)*
- **Claim:** In contexts carrying a power or dependency imbalance — a boss, a landlord, a mutual professional or social contact — giving an explicit reason for disengaging can be used by the other party as leverage or grounds for retaliation, making silent disengagement the lower-risk option specifically in those cases.
- **Mechanism:**
  - Some disengagement happens between people who share a power-imbalanced context beyond the interaction itself (workplace-adjacent, housing-adjacent, mutual professional/social overlap)
  - An explicit reason gives that person specific, attributable content they can dispute, reframe, or use against the discloser in the shared context
  - Silent disengagement withholds that attributable content, reducing the surface area available for retaliation
- **Evidence:** Structural inference from general power-imbalance/retaliation-risk reasoning; no case-specific citation available. *Confidence: plausible-but-unverified, low-confidence — genuinely useful mainly as narrow supporting material.*
- **Second-Order Effect:** None.
- **Impact:** Breadth: narrow — only the subset of disputed cases carrying an independent power/dependency relationship. Depth: potentially significant within that subset. Comparative: real but narrow; supplementary, not a pillar.

---

### 9. Prompt Exit Beats Prolonged Low-Quality Contact
- **Claim:** A culture that expects effortful justification before disengaging discourages people from disengaging promptly, keeping them in low-quality or unwanted contact for longer than a norm that treats quiet exit as acceptable would.
- **Mechanism:**
  - If ending contact carries a social cost (composing and delivering a justified explanation, managing the reaction), people facing that cost have an incentive to postpone disengaging rather than pay it immediately
  - That postponement keeps both people in contact neither wants to continue at full intensity — reduced engagement, slower replies, avoidance — for a stretch of time before the eventual disengagement finally happens
  - A norm that doesn't demand a costly justification removes that incentive to delay, so disengagement tends to happen closer to the moment interest actually ended
- **Evidence:** Structural inference from standard cost-benefit/delay-incentive reasoning applied to this specific social behaviour. *Confidence: plausible-but-unverified — a reasonable extrapolation from a general incentive-structure principle, not a direct study of dating/social disengagement timing.*
- **Second-Order Effect:** None.
- **Impact:** Breadth: wherever the cost of exiting exceeds the cost of quietly disengaging — per Arguments 3 and 5, most low-investment cases. Depth: moderate — the harm here is time spent in degraded contact rather than a sharp injury. Comparative: complements Argument 4 without duplicating it — Argument 4 is about honesty, this is about timing; both point the same direction (faster resolution beats a hedged delay).
